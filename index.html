Replenishment Context Engine

We are not building a better AI model. We build the information environment an AI copilot needs before it recommends a stock move: the right products, live and committed inventory, constraints, and a source for every number.

Business rules come from the systems and from the planner's own policies. The engine applies them so the AI only proposes actions that can actually happen.

  • FitsDelta truck, free at 21:00: 26 pallets × 40 cases = 1,040 capacity, enough for 900.
  • FitsKamloops truck free at 20:30, 4 h 45 m to Burnaby. Arrives 01:15, before store receiving opens at 06:00.
  • RuleDowntown Granville has no night receiving (23:00 to 06:00). Its 340 cases go first on the Delta run.
  • RuleTransfers over $1,500 need regional manager approval. Kamloops leg is $2,100.
  • BlockedDiverting the Victoria ferry order breaks a customer commitment. Not offered without director approval.
  • InfoSupplier lead time 48 h: an expedited purchase order helps Saturday, not tomorrow morning.

The AI receives a short, structured brief instead of thousands of rows. Every line carries its source and timestamp, so the answer can cite them and the planner can check them. Provenance follows the W3C PROV model: entity, activity, agent.

Problem

One user, one painful moment

Who: inventory and replenishment planners at mid-to-large grocery and convenience chains (50+ stores, 2+ distribution centres). When: demand on a fast-moving item breaks from forecast because of weather, a promotion or a local event, and stock has to move within hours.

Today's workflow (hypothesis to confirm in interviews): the planner sees a sales alert, opens store stock, opens the warehouse system, checks open orders, phones or emails the transport desk, checks the approval policy, then builds the transfer by hand. Five or more systems, each with different product IDs and update times. AI copilots fail here for the same reason the planner is slow: the facts are scattered and some are stale.

Why it matters: research on retail out-of-stocks has long found average shelf out-of-stock rates around 8%, with retailers losing roughly 4% of sales as a result (Gruen, Corsten & Bharadwaj, 2002). Industry estimates put global losses from out-of-stocks and overstocks in the trillion-dollar range (IHL Group). Verify exact figures and years from the original reports before citing in the deck.

Solution

What the engine does that SAP does not do alone

SAP already provides much of the foundation: Business Data Cloud and Datasphere publish data products, the SAP Knowledge Graph describes what business objects mean and how they relate, and Joule gives users an AI assistant. We build on these rather than replace them. Our layer adds what a copilot needs at the moment of a decision:

  • Decision-time freshness checks. Each fact type has a maximum age. Stale facts are re-queried or flagged, never silently used.
  • Committed vs. free stock. On-hand inventory is netted against open orders before the AI sees it.
  • Operational constraints and policy. Truck capacity, receiving hours, approval thresholds and protected orders limit which actions the AI may propose.
  • Per-claim provenance. Every number in the answer links to a system, record and timestamp (W3C PROV).
  • Compact context. A brief of a few hundred tokens instead of a table dump, which lowers cost and reduces the chance the model fixes on irrelevant rows (Lewis et al., 2020, on retrieval-augmented generation).

Positioning: a packaged Joule agent for retail replenishment, delivered by SAP or a partner, not a competing data platform.

Adoption

Land small, prove it, expand

  1. Shadow mode, weeks 1–4. One region, one weather-sensitive category (bottled water and ice). The engine recommends; planners decide as usual. We log both.
  2. Assisted mode, weeks 5–8. Planners act from the engine's drafts and approve in Joule.
  3. Expand. Add promotion-driven categories, then more regions, once the pilot meets its targets.

Pilot metrics: out-of-stock hours on spike days, time from alert to approved transfer, share of recommendations accepted, and errors caught (stale or committed stock the copilot alone would have used).

Evidence we generated ourselves

Evaluation results

We took 20 real demand spikes from the M5 Walmart store-sales data and added synthetic warehouse, order and truck tables. Each scenario hides traps: an out-of-date snapshot, shipped and cancelled orders mixed with open ones, stock counted in single units, trucks in maintenance or arriving too late, and costs that need approval. Each has one known correct answer. The same model (Claude Sonnet) answered every question twice: once with the raw tables, once with the context packet.

Model inputCorrectUnsafe plansInput tokens (est.)
Raw tables (all relevant tables included)19 / 190~4,600
Context packet19 / 190~760

What it shows: when a strong model is handed every relevant table, it reasons correctly. The packet gives the same answers with about 6× less input, which means lower cost and room for larger questions. The demo's failure case comes from the step before: a plain search often fetches the stock table but not open orders or trucks. Choosing and checking the right facts is the engine's job.

Two of 40 runs were not completed. Token counts are estimated as characters ÷ 4. Next test: a smaller model and larger raw data pulls, to find where raw tables stop working. Code and data steps are in the project's README.

Evidence still to gather

Validation plan

  • Planner interviews (5–8). Supply chain and inventory managers via LinkedIn, alumni and local grocery chains. Ask them to walk through the last demand spike: which systems, how long, what went wrong. Turn answers into a quote slide and a timed workflow.
  • Harder evaluation. Rerun the with/without test with a smaller model and with category-wide raw data pulls, and add retrieval: let the plain copilot choose which tables to fetch.
  • Wendy's / Palantir stays as one slide of market proof: a large food operator paid to connect supply chain data into one operational picture, and a human approved the system's proposed actions. That is our model too.
